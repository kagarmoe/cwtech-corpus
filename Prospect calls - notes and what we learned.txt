NORTHWIND TECHNOLOGIES
Prospect calls -- notes and what we learned

Prepared by:  John Mercer
Date:         April 12, 2010
Status:       Internal

I said I would make a dozen calls before we revisited the services
draft. I have talked to 14 people, including three people who are not
prospects but know the market well enough that I wanted their reaction.

The short version: I think there is a business here.

I also think Alice was right that "we run your infrastructure" means
something different to everybody who hears it.

WHAT PEOPLE RESPONDED TO

Nobody cares that we can install Linux. Everybody assumes somebody can
install Linux.

What gets attention is having one company responsible for the boring
operational work that currently falls between their hosting company,
whoever built the application, and whoever in the office knows the root
password.

Several people described some version of:

    Hosting company says the server is up.
    Developer says the application works.
    Customer says the site is broken.

Nobody owns the problem in the middle.

That is the part of the pitch that works.

"Don't hire a sysadmin" also gets attention, although I need to be
careful with it. Companies with a good systems person do not want to
hear that they don't need one. Companies without one know perfectly well
that they don't have one.

WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY HAVE

This is messier than the services draft makes it sound.

Very few of these companies have an "environment" that somebody designed
all at once.

Things I heard about:

    - One or two dedicated servers at a hosting company
    - A database on the same machine as the application
    - A second server added when the first one got too slow
    - Backups that somebody believes are running
    - A server in the office that still does something important
    - Developers with production passwords
    - DNS registered by whoever built the first web site
    - A firewall nobody wants to change because nobody remembers why
    - Old machines nobody is sure can be turned off
    - Monitoring that means a customer calls when the site is down

Alice's "look first" rule is going to matter more than I thought.

We are not going to get many clean environments handed to us.

PRICE

Everybody asked.

Nobody wants hourly administration as the main arrangement. They hear
"consultant" and start worrying about whether they can afford to call us
when something goes wrong.

A predictable monthly number gets a much better reaction.

That does not mean one flat price for everything. The environments are
too different.

My current thought, not a decision:

    Monthly management fee based on what we agree to manage.

    Projects and major changes quoted separately.

    Hardware, colo and other direct costs passed through or separately
    itemized.

We need to figure out what the management fee is based on. Number of
servers is easy to explain but may be a bad measure of work.

WHAT THEY EXPECT US TO BE RESPONSIBLE FOR

This is the hard part.

If we say we manage the servers, customers hear:

    "When something is broken, I call Northwind."

They do not care very much whether the cause turns out to be Apache, a
switch, a disk, a database query, somebody's application release, or the
hosting provider.

I still think this is the opportunity.

Alice points out that it is also how two people end up responsible for
everything anybody has ever plugged into a network.

We need language that lets us own the investigation without
automatically owning the cause.

That may be the most important thing we have to settle before we sign
anybody.

RESPONSE TIME

Everyone asked some version of "What happens when it breaks at 2am?"

Nobody demanded a five-nines SLA from a two-person company.

They did want to know that somebody would answer.

There is probably a difference between:

    We received the alert and are looking at it.

and:

    We guarantee it will be fixed in 30 minutes.

We can probably promise the first before we can promise the second.

Alice and I need to work out what is physically possible before I put
numbers around this.

COLOCATION

Alice got preliminary numbers from three Denver-area facilities.

No reason to sign for space yet.

Several prospects already have hosting arrangements and would rather
have us manage what they have than move immediately. That may be good
for us financially, although it means inheriting more strange
environments.

For customers who need new equipment, we will need a preferred facility
and a repeatable way to buy and install hardware. Not yet.

PROMISING CONVERSATIONS

Three worth following up.

1. HARTWELL & REED

About 35 employees. Architecture and engineering firm.

They have a Windows-heavy office environment and a small public-facing
Linux system maintained by the consultant who built their web site.

They mostly want somebody local they can call.

I don't think this is our first customer. Too much desktop and office IT
in what they mean by "infrastructure." Could be business later if we
decide we want that work.

2. SUMMIT TICKETING

Online registration and ticket sales for small events and regional
venues.

Seven employees plus contractors.

They have two leased Linux servers with a hosting provider. One runs the
web application and database. The other was added last year for reasons
I do not completely understand yet. Their primary developer has been
handling deployments, server maintenance and outages.

They are growing and he does not want to be the operations person
anymore.

They had an outage last month that lasted several hours. Their hosting
provider said both servers were reachable. The developer was
unavailable. The owner ended up calling everybody she knew who might
understand Linux.

She understood our pitch immediately.

She wants us to come look at what they have before we talk about moving
anything.

This is the strongest lead.

3. CLEAR CREEK ANALYTICS

Small environmental data company.

They have several Linux workstations and servers doing data processing,
plus customer-facing systems at a hosting provider.

Interesting technical work and probably a good fit eventually.

Right now they mostly want help with a storage problem and a server
migration. Sounds more like a project than an ongoing managed
relationship.

Still worth talking to.

WHAT I WAS WRONG ABOUT

I thought the first sale would probably include new servers and colo
because that is what I am used to selling.

It may not.

The more immediate market may be companies that already have
infrastructure and need somebody to take operational responsibility for
it.

That is less revenue up front but also means we do not have to spend
money on equipment or space before we have customers.

I also underestimated how little these companies know about what they
already have.

If we take over an existing environment, inventory is not administrative
paperwork. It is part of the work.

WHAT I THINK WE SHOULD DO NEXT

Follow up with Summit Ticketing.

Do not propose a migration yet.

Alice and I should meet with them, get access, and inspect the two
servers and whatever documentation they have.

Then we can answer three questions:

    1. What is actually there?
    2. What would we be willing to take responsibility for?
    3. What would it cost us to do that responsibly?

If we cannot answer those questions on a two-server environment, we are
not ready to sell this service.

Separately, Alice and I need to settle:

    - what an initial environment review consists of
    - what we record about systems we manage
    - what "we respond" actually means after hours
    - how a monthly management price relates to the work involved

I am not changing the services draft yet. Fourteen conversations are
enough to tell me where the problems are, not enough to pretend we have
solved them.
