NORTHWIND TECHNOLOGIES
The disk check that could not fail

Prepared by:  Wes Okafor
Reviewed by:  Priya Raghunathan
Date:         13 November 2017
Status:       Internal

This one is mine.  I am writing it a month late, with the fix already in, because
I wanted to be able to say what we had done about it rather than what we were
going to.  The process we agreed in June worked exactly as designed and did not
help.


WHAT I CHANGED

On 20 September I changed two things in check_disk.

The first: df -P columns move when a device name is long enough that df wraps
the line, and we read column 5.  I asked df for the fields instead --
--output=pcent,size -- so the percent is always field 1.  Tidy.  Since the field
can then never be missing, I took the empty case out of the guard below it.

The second was a real bug and older than me.  The line read

    line=`ssh host "df -P $mount" | tail -1` || { cannot reach; exit 2; }

and the status of that is tail's status.  tail does not fail.  That || has never
fired once since Alice wrote it in 2010.  I moved the tail to the far side of
the ssh so that what we test is ssh.

That second change is correct.  I would make it again.


WHAT HAPPENED

df --output arrived in coreutils 8.21, in February 2013.  We manage six
operating system releases and four of them are older than that:

    CentOS 5.4     no        CentOS 5.5     no
    Debian 5       no        Ubuntu 8.04    no
    Ubuntu 14.04   yes       Debian 8       yes

On the four, df printed its usage to stderr and exited 1.  Here is the rest of
it, and every step is something I did on purpose:

    df exits 1                  but it is now piped to tail on the far side,
                                and what ssh reports back is tail's status, 0
    so ssh returns 0            and the || does not fire.  I moved that pipe.
    stderr is 2>/dev/null       so the usage message is gone
    awk over nothing            so the percent is the empty string
    case "" in *[!0-9]*         does not match.  I removed the '' case in the
                                same change, because --output made it impossible
    [ "" -ge 90 ]               is not false.  It is an error.
    and `if` reads an error     as false, so we skip the critical branch
    the last line is echo       and echo exits 0

    OK.

The fix I was proudest of is the one that carried it.  Moving the tail to the
far side was right, and it moved the mask one hop instead of removing it.

    From 20 September to 12 October, every disk check on web1.summit,
    web2.summit, ccweb1, ccdata1, ccdata2, bw-web1, bw-db1 and hr-www
    reported OK.  Every single run.  Whatever the disk was doing.

That is eight hosts and sixteen checks, green for twenty-two days, on the oldest
and most important customers we have.


HOW WE FOUND IT

Not from an alert.  There cannot be an alert; that is the whole problem.

web1.summit's /var was at 88% on 12 September and Priya had it written down.
She expected it to cross 90 inside a month and went to nwtrend to see when it
had first warned, so she could tell Summit how fast it was moving.

It had never warned.  Not since 20 September -- never, in seven years.

    She asked me when I had last touched check_disk.  I said 20 September.

Then she ran it by hand and got `/ % full`, and a complaint from the shell about
an illegal number, and an exit status of zero.

Nothing bad happened in the twenty-two days.  We got lucky and I want that
written down as luck and not as mitigation.


THE PART THAT BOTHERS ME

Priya's condition from June is that I do not change a check that is currently
catching something without telling her first.

**I told her.**  She read it.  We both looked at it and neither of us thought
about coreutils 8.21, because both of us do our thinking on machines that are
younger than it.

The review worked.  The reviewer and the author had the same blind spot, which
is what happens when two people from the same small company review each other,
and no amount of telling each other first fixes it.

The tests are worse than that, and this is the part I have thought about most.

They did not pass.  Three of them failed.

The stub that stands in for ssh matched on the literal string "df -P /var" and
my new command no longer said that, so I changed the stub to dispatch on the
mountpoint.  The canned df output was two lines, a header and a row, and the
tail now runs on the far side where the stub does not honour it, so I made the
canned output the one line tail would have returned.  And I rewrote those lines
into the layout --output=pcent,size produces.

Then sixty-three tests passed and I committed it.

I want to be accurate about what I did there.  I did not ignore a failing test.
I did the thing that looked like maintenance: the command changed, so the
fixture changed.  It is the same edit you make when you rename a field.  The
difference is that a fixture is a claim about what the world returns, and I had
just changed my claim about the world to agree with a command I had only ever
run on one machine.

The suite now asserted that every host we manage prints a percent and a size
and nothing else.  Four of the six do not print anything at all.  No test could
have known, because every machine in the suite is a file I wrote.

One of the three was "unreachable host fails", written by Alice in 2010 for a
reason that has nothing to do with any of this, and it failed for exactly the
right reason: with the empty case gone from the guard, a host that answers with
nothing now returns OK.  I read that failure, understood it as the stub being
out of date, and edited the stub.

It was not the stub being out of date.  It was the test telling me.


THE OLDER PROBLEM THIS IS

In April 2011, in her first month, Priya asked: "What happens if a check is
wrong and reports OK forever?"

The answer she got, and wrote down, was: "Nothing.  We would not know."

That has been in the file for six years.  Nobody solved it because it never
happened, and then it happened, and we found out because one person had a number
written down and went to look at how fast it was moving.


WHAT WE HAVE DONE

**nwtry.**  Runs a check against one host of each operating system release we
actually manage -- six of them, CentOS 5.4 through Debian 8 -- and shows what
each one returns.  It takes about four seconds.

    Eleven years of releases, and I had been testing on the newest one because
    it is the one in front of me.

The rule: a change to a check gets run through nwtry and the output goes in the
commit message.  Not a policy document.  Four seconds and a paste.

**nwcheck keeps stderr apart from stdout**, and a check that exits 0 while the
shell is complaining is not OK.

    "[: Illegal number:" was in the output of every one of those runs.  We
    captured it.  We wrote it to the history file.  We filed it under OK and
    nobody ever read it, because nothing we have ever shows you an OK line.

This is the one that would have caught it, on the first run, on 20 September.
It is nine lines.

**nwtrend --stale.**  Lists checks that have not reported anything other than OK
for longer than they usually go, comparing each check against its own history.

    It would not have caught this.  web1.summit disk has never once been non-OK
    in seven years, so there is no cadence to compare it against and --stale
    skips it.  It answers Priya's question for a check that used to be right and
    stopped.  For a check that has never once fired, we are where we were in
    2011, and I would rather write that down than let the entry in deferred look
    tidier than it is.

    Twenty lines of awk, six years late, and a partial answer.

**Reverted the rest of the change.**  check_disk counts columns out of df -P
again, which is what I was trying to get away from and which works everywhere.
The tail stays on the far side; that part was right.  The comment above the line
says why, at length, so that the next person who finds it untidy reads the
reason before they fix it.


WHAT I HAVE NOT DONE

Anything about the stub tests.  They are good tests for what they test and I am
not going to build a machine room of virtual CentOS 5 boxes for a six-person
company.  nwtry against real hosts is the cheaper answer and it is honest about
what it is.
